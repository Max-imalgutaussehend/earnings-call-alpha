Does segment-level sentiment beat whole-transcript sentiment?

A falsifiable test on 16 real, first-party earnings-call transcripts: whether the dispersion of FinBERT sentiment across a call's Q&A segments predicts abnormal returns beyond what the whole-transcript average already explains.

n = 16 calls 5 tickers · 4 sectors JPM · BAC · MSFT · CAT · XOM FinBERT · Fama-French · PSR
01 · Hypothesis

A falsifiable claim, stated before looking at results

H1: the dispersion of sentiment across a call's segments (max − min segment sentiment, and specifically the sentiment of the segment covering the company's largest business line) has incremental predictive power for the 1-day and 3-day abnormal return around the call, beyond what whole-transcript sentiment alone explains.

Why this, not another metric

Most quant portfolios show price-only signals; most IB-style analyses show no statistical validation. This sits between the two — a qualitative artifact (an earnings call) tested with a quant-style falsifiable claim, explicit backtest, and transaction costs. A null result here is treated as a complete, reportable finding, not a failed run.

02 · Method

Six steps, each independently auditable

1 · Segment each call into prepared-remarks blocks and analyst Q&A exchanges (rule-based, not learned — every split can be hand-checked). 2 · Score each segment with FinBERT, chosen over an LLM judge for determinism and reproducibility. 3 · Compute whole-transcript sentiment, segment dispersion, and core-segment sentiment (keyword-matched to the firm's largest reported business line). 4 · Measure Fama-French market-model abnormal returns for [0,+1] and [0,+3] trading days. 5 · Test with a nested regression isolating dispersion's incremental R². 6 · Backtest a walk-forward long/short sort on the signal, scored with the Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio rather than a raw Sharpe ratio.

03 · Sample

16 calls, sourced first-party only

Every transcript is either an SEC 8-K exhibit or a PDF/HTML transcript published directly by the company's own investor-relations site — never a third-party aggregator, to keep provenance fully auditable. Apple, Wells Fargo, P&G, and Johnson & Johnson were each evaluated and excluded for not meeting that bar.

04 · Regression test

H1 is not supported — and getting weaker, not stronger

A nested OLS regression compares whole-transcript sentiment alone against whole-transcript sentiment + dispersion. The number that matters is the incremental R² dispersion adds, and whether its coefficient clears significance.

07 · Limitations, stated up front

What this is not

01

Small, non-random sample. 16 calls across 5 tickers, curated for transcript availability — not a random draw from the market. Results don't generalize beyond this sample.

02

No multiple-testing correction across the two return windows tested (1-day, 3-day). At this sample size that correction would only push already-insignificant p-values further from significance.

03

Core-segment keywords use present-day knowledge of each firm's largest business line, which may not match what was true in an older quarter.

04

Daily closes, not intraday. Event-window returns are coarser than the sub-minute reaction studied in some market-microstructure literature.

05

This is a research prototype, not a claim of a deployable trading signal. The backtest is illustrative of methodology — walk-forward split, transaction costs, Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio — not a performance claim.
